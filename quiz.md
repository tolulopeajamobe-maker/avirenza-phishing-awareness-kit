# Phishing Awareness Quiz
**AVIRENZA Cybersecurity Awareness Kit**  
**Audience:** College administrative staff  
**Suggested time:** 8–10 minutes  
**Instructions:** Choose the best answer for each question. The examples are fictional. Do not click or visit any example links.

---

## Questions

### 1. A message appears with the display name “Principal's Office” and asks you to buy gift cards urgently. What should you do first?

A. Buy the cards because the display name is familiar.  
B. Reply to the email and ask whether the request is genuine.  
C. Verify the request using a known, separate contact method before taking action.  
D. Forward the email to several colleagues to ask what they think.

### 2. An email says your account will be disabled in 30 minutes unless you sign in through a link. Which detail is the strongest reason not to use that link?

A. The email uses a greeting rather than your name.  
B. The link destination is an unfamiliar domain that has not been verified.  
C. The message arrived during working hours.  
D. The message is shorter than other IT emails.

### 3. Which request should trigger the strongest pause and verification?

A. A colleague asks where the printer paper is stored.  
B. A staff member asks you to confirm a meeting room booking.  
C. A message asks you to bypass the normal finance approval process and transfer money.  
D. IT announces a planned maintenance window through the established staff channel.

### 4. You receive a delivery notice asking for a small redelivery fee, but you are not sure whether you ordered anything. What is the safest next step?

A. Pay the fee because the amount is small.  
B. Open the link only on your phone.  
C. Reply and provide your home address to confirm delivery.  
D. Check independently through the carrier's official app or website, if you are expecting a parcel.

### 5. A colleague sends an unexpected attachment that asks you to sign in with your college password. The email mentions a real meeting you attended. What is the best interpretation?

A. The real meeting detail proves the sender is genuine.  
B. Personal details can make a spear-phishing message convincing; verify separately and do not enter credentials.  
C. It is safe if the attachment has a familiar file icon.  
D. You should sign in first and change your password afterward.

### 6. **Tricky scenario:** You receive a message from a verified-looking address that asks you to change a supplier's bank details. The message contains no spelling mistakes and uses the correct project name. What should you do?

A. Process the change because the address and project name look correct.  
B. Reply to the same email thread asking the sender to confirm.  
C. Follow the organisation's independent supplier-change verification and approval procedure.  
D. Process a small test payment to see whether the account works.

### 7. Which statement about password managers is most accurate?

A. They make every website safe to use.  
B. They store or generate unique passwords so you do not have to reuse or remember each one; use an organisation-approved manager.  
C. They mean you no longer need multi-factor authentication.  
D. They are safe only when the same password is used for every account.

### 8. What is multi-factor authentication (MFA)?

A. Using the same password twice to confirm it.  
B. Changing your password every morning.  
C. Using two or more different types of proof to confirm your identity, such as a password plus an authenticator approval.  
D. Sending your password to IT so they can confirm your identity.

### 9. **Tricky scenario:** An authenticator prompt appears on your phone, but you are not trying to sign in. Someone then calls claiming to be IT and asks you to read out the one-time code. What should you do?

A. Approve the prompt because IT called.  
B. Read the code only if the caller knows your job title.  
C. Deny the unexpected prompt, do not share the code, and report the incident through the known IT channel.  
D. Ignore the prompt and call the number shown in the suspicious email.

### 10. You accidentally clicked a suspicious link and entered your password. What is the best response?

A. Say nothing unless you notice money missing.  
B. Delete the email and hope nothing happens.  
C. Report it immediately through the approved IT route and follow their instructions to secure the account.  
D. Forward the link to coworkers so they can check whether it works.

---

# Answer Key and Explanations

## 1. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** A display name can be copied or spoofed. It does not prove who sent the email.
- **B — Incorrect:** Replying keeps you inside the potentially compromised conversation and may reach the attacker.
- **C — Correct:** Verify unusual financial or gift-card requests using a known phone number or approved internal channel, not contact details supplied in the message.
- **D — Incorrect:** Forwarding to colleagues can spread the suspicious message. Use the approved reporting route instead.

## 2. Correct answer: B

- **A — Incorrect:** A generic greeting can be a clue, but it is not enough by itself to establish that a message is malicious.
- **B — Correct:** An unfamiliar, unverified destination is a strong reason not to use the link. Navigate to the known service independently.
- **C — Incorrect:** Phishing can arrive at any time, including normal business hours.
- **D — Incorrect:** Message length is not a reliable test. Short messages can be legitimate or malicious.

## 3. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** This is an ordinary low-risk workplace question, though context still matters.
- **B — Incorrect:** A routine room-booking question is not inherently suspicious.
- **C — Correct:** Requests to transfer money while bypassing established controls are high risk. Stop and verify through the normal approval process.
- **D — Incorrect:** A maintenance notice through an established channel is more consistent with normal internal communication, though unexpected links should still be checked.

## 4. Correct answer: D

- **A — Incorrect:** A small amount can be used to steal payment details or test a stolen card.
- **B — Incorrect:** A phone does not make a suspicious link safe.
- **C — Incorrect:** Do not send personal information to an unverified sender.
- **D — Correct:** Open the carrier's official app or type its known website address yourself. Check only if you are actually expecting a parcel.

## 5. Correct answer: B

- **A — Incorrect:** Attackers may obtain meeting details from shared calendars, compromised accounts, public posts or other sources. A correct detail is not proof of identity.
- **B — Correct:** This is a common spear-phishing technique. Confirm the request using a separate, known channel and do not enter credentials into an unexpected file.
- **C — Incorrect:** File icons and familiar-looking names can be copied. They do not establish safety.
- **D — Incorrect:** Entering credentials first may already expose the account. Do not interact with the suspicious attachment.

## 6. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** A familiar address and accurate project details can still be used in a compromised-account or impersonation attack.
- **B — Incorrect:** If the sender's account or thread is compromised, a reply may go to the attacker. Verify independently.
- **C — Correct:** Supplier bank-detail changes require the organisation's established, independent verification and approval controls, regardless of how convincing the email looks.
- **D — Incorrect:** A test payment still sends money to an unverified account and does not validate the supplier's identity.

## 7. Correct answer: B

- **A — Incorrect:** A password manager cannot make a fraudulent website legitimate or prevent every type of attack.
- **B — Correct:** It helps create and store unique passwords and can reduce password reuse. Staff should use the tool approved by their organisation.
- **C — Incorrect:** MFA adds another layer of protection and should still be used where available.
- **D — Incorrect:** Reusing one password increases risk. A manager helps avoid that behaviour.

## 8. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** Repeating the same password is still one type of factor.
- **B — Incorrect:** Frequent password changes alone are not MFA.
- **C — Correct:** MFA asks for more than one kind of proof, such as something you know (password) and something you have (authenticator app or security key).
- **D — Incorrect:** Staff should never send passwords to IT. Legitimate support staff should not ask for them.

## 9. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** An unexpected prompt may mean someone else has your password and is trying to sign in. Do not approve it.
- **B — Incorrect:** A caller knowing your job title does not authenticate them. Never disclose one-time codes.
- **C — Correct:** Deny the prompt, keep the code private and report through a known IT route. If you suspect your password is exposed, follow IT's account-securing instructions promptly.
- **D — Incorrect:** Do not use contact details from the suspicious email. Use a known directory, saved helpdesk contact or established internal channel.

## 10. Correct answer: C

- **A — Incorrect:** Waiting can give an attacker time to use the credentials or access the account.
- **B — Incorrect:** Deleting the message does not secure the account and may remove evidence IT needs.
- **C — Correct:** Report immediately, even if you feel embarrassed. IT can advise on password changes, session revocation and other containment steps.
- **D — Incorrect:** Sharing the link spreads the risk. Do not test suspicious links or ask coworkers to open them.

---

## Trainer scoring guide

- **9–10 correct:** Strong understanding. Reinforce reporting and verification habits.
- **7–8 correct:** Good foundation. Review the missed topics during a short follow-up.
- **5–6 correct:** Needs reinforcement. Repeat the practical examples and reporting steps.
- **0–4 correct:** Provide additional coaching and a repeat knowledge check.

**Important:** A quiz score measures knowledge, not necessarily behaviour. Pair quiz results with safe reporting metrics and staff feedback. Do not use scores to shame or punish individuals.

## Fictional-content and ethics statement

All scenarios and names in this quiz are fictional. Any domains used in the wider kit use the reserved `.invalid` suffix and are not real destinations. No real person or organisation was contacted or targeted.
